Link Bookmark Component

January 20, 2026

The link bookmark appears inline within your writing, offering readers a calm, contextual reference without disrupting the flow of your prose. Below are the two primary variants.

Variant 1 — Text-First

Use when the link's content speaks for itself, when no meaningful image exists, or when you want maximum restraint. Feels like a refined footnote.

newyorker.com

The Morality of Wilderness: Why Solitude Still Matters in a Connected Age

A meditation on the necessity of disconnection, and what we lose when every moment becomes a potential broadcast.
aeon.co

What Ancient Libraries Teach Us About Digital Preservation

When to use text-first

Academic articles, essays, documentation, or any link where the title and source provide sufficient context. Also ideal when the source's preview image would feel promotional or off-tone.

Variant 2 — Image-Accented

Use when a visual adds genuine context—architecture, art, photography, places. The image supports; it never dominates.

archdaily.com

Tadao Ando's Church of the Light: Thirty Years of Sacred Minimalism

How a concrete box in Osaka became one of the most influential religious spaces of the modern era.
theparisreview.org

The Art of the Sentence: An Interview with Marilynne Robinson

When to use image-accented

Articles about visual subjects—architecture, art, design, travel, photography. The thumbnail should feel like an illustration that hints at the content, not a promotional banner.

In-Context Example

How the bookmark sits within actual prose:

The question of what makes a space sacred has occupied architects for centuries. Few have answered it as directly as Tadao Ando, whose work strips away ornament to reveal something essential about light, material, and presence.

archdaily.com

Tadao Ando's Church of the Light: Thirty Years of Sacred Minimalism

How a concrete box in Osaka became one of the most influential religious spaces of the modern era.

His Church of the Light, completed in 1989, achieves this through radical economy. A cruciform slot cut into the concrete altar wall admits daylight in the shape of a cross—no stained glass, no gilding, just light itself becoming symbol.

Design Specifications

Typography

  • Title: Libre Baskerville, 17px, weight 400, line-height 1.45
  • Source: Inter, 13px, color #8A8A80
  • Description: Inter, 14px, color #6B6B63, line-height 1.55

Colors

  • Background: #FFFFFF (elevated surface)
  • Border: #EEEEE9 (default) → #D4D3C8 (selected)
  • Hover bg: #F7F6F3
  • Selected bg: #F0EFE9

Spacing

  • Padding: 20px
  • Border radius: 8px
  • Margin (vertical): 24px
  • Thumbnail: 88×88px (64×64px compact)

Interaction

  • Hover: Background warms, border darkens slightly
  • Selected: Soft olive tint, focus ring at 15% opacity
  • Transition: 200ms ease
